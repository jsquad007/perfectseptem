In the previous article, we asked [Did Jesus Ever Say, “I Am God”?](https://www.perfectseptem.com/posts/2026-08-28-jesus-never-says-the-exact-words-i-am-god).

We saw that the question is too narrow. The Gospels do not give us a single sentence in which Jesus simply says, “I am God.” Instead, they present us with a much larger question:

**What did Jesus claim the authority to do?**

This is particularly important when we read the Gospels through Jewish eyes.

Jesus lived and taught within the world of the Old Testament Scriptures. His listeners knew that certain things belonged uniquely to God: forgiving sins, judging humanity, ruling creation, establishing the Sabbath and dwelling among His people.

Yet the Gospels repeatedly show Jesus exercising authority in these areas.

The question is whether this is simply the authority of a prophet acting on God's behalf, or whether Jesus is claiming something far greater.

## Who can forgive sins?

One of the clearest examples occurs in Mark 2.

A paralysed man is brought to Jesus. Before healing him, Jesus says:

> “Son, your sins are forgiven.”
> — Mark 2:5 (NRSVA)

The scribes immediately object.

Their reasoning is important:

> “Why does this fellow speak in this way? It is blasphemy! Who can forgive sins but God alone?”
> — Mark 2:7 (NRSVA)

Their statement reflects a fundamental Jewish understanding.

A person can forgive someone for an offence committed against that person. But sin ultimately offends God.

If someone sins against me, I can forgive that person.

But if someone sins against God, I cannot simply declare that person's debt before God cancelled by my own authority.

Jesus nevertheless does exactly this.

He then heals the paralysed man to demonstrate that **His claim to forgive sins is backed by an extraordinary, visible act of authority**:

> “Stand up, take your mat and go to your home.”
> — Mark 2:11 (NRSVA)

The healing is therefore not simply an act of compassion, nor is it presented as the thing that makes forgiveness possible.

Jesus has made an **invisible claim**: that the man's sins are forgiven.

The scribes cannot see whether that claim has actually taken effect.

So Jesus performs something they **can** see: He immediately commands a paralysed man to get up, take his mat and go home.

The greater the claim, the greater the significance of the sign.

Jesus is not merely demonstrating that He can perform a relatively ordinary act of healing. He is demonstrating, through an unmistakable and extraordinary miracle, that He possesses the authority He has just claimed.

The miracle is therefore presented as evidence of **authority**.

Jesus claims an authority His critics understand as belonging to God.

## “The Son of Man has authority”

Jesus frequently refers to Himself as **the Son of Man**.

This title deserves much more attention than it usually receives.

In Daniel 7, Daniel sees a figure described as “one like a son of man” who comes with the clouds of heaven.

He is presented before the Ancient One and receives:

> “dominion and glory and kingship”

and all peoples and nations are said to serve him (Daniel 7:13-14).

The figure is not merely another ordinary human ruler.

He is given an everlasting dominion and a kingdom that will not pass away.

When Jesus uses the title “Son of Man”, therefore, His Jewish audience has reason to hear more than the phrase “human being”.

This becomes particularly significant when Jesus says that the **Son of Man has authority to forgive sins**.

The title connects Jesus' authority with the extraordinary heavenly figure of Daniel's vision.

## Jesus and the Sabbath

Another remarkable example occurs when Jesus' disciples are criticised for picking grain on the Sabbath.

Jesus responds by discussing the actions of David and the priests, and then concludes:

> “the Son of Man is lord even of the sabbath.”
> — Mark 2:28 (NRSVA)

The Sabbath was not simply another Jewish custom.

It was established by God in creation and incorporated into the covenant law.

The commandment begins:

> “Remember the sabbath day, and keep it holy.”
> — Exodus 20:8 (NRSVA)

Jesus does not merely explain what the Sabbath means.

He calls Himself **Lord of the Sabbath**.

That is an extraordinary claim.

If the Sabbath belongs to God, what does it mean for Jesus to claim lordship over it?

The context matters. Jesus is not abolishing the Sabbath. He is asserting authority over its proper interpretation and purpose.

Once again, Jesus places Himself in a position of authority over something established by God.

## Something greater than the Temple

Jesus makes an even more striking statement in Matthew 12.

After discussing the actions of David and the priests, He says:

> “I tell you, something greater than the temple is here.”
> — Matthew 12:6 (NRSVA)

For a Jewish audience, this is an extraordinary statement.

The Temple was the centre of Israel's worship and was associated with the presence of God among His people.

Jesus does not simply claim to be another teacher within the Temple.

He says that **something greater than the Temple is here**.

And the “something” is not an abstract teaching.

It is Jesus Himself.

The claim becomes even more significant when we remember how the New Testament later develops the theme.

John says that the eternal Word became flesh and “lived among us” (John 1:14).

Paul describes Christ as the one in whom “the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily” (Colossians 2:9).

The presence of God is no longer centred only upon a building.

It is encountered in the person of Christ.

## Jesus and the divine bridegroom

There is another claim that can easily be overlooked.

When people ask Jesus why His disciples do not fast, He responds by speaking about a **bridegroom**.

He says:

> “The wedding guests cannot fast while the bridegroom is with them, can they?”
> — Mark 2:19 (NRSVA)

Why is this significant?

Throughout the Old Testament, the relationship between God and Israel is repeatedly described using marriage imagery.

God is Israel's husband.

Isaiah says:

> “For your Maker is your husband, the LORD of hosts is his name.”
> — Isaiah 54:5 (NRSVA)

The prophets use the same imagery when describing Israel's unfaithfulness.

Jesus therefore steps into an image that the Old Testament uses for **God's covenant relationship with His people**.

He identifies Himself as the bridegroom.

This becomes even more significant later in the New Testament, when Christ is described as the bridegroom and the Church as His bride (Ephesians 5:25-32; Revelation 19:7).

Jesus is not simply another prophet asking Israel to return to her husband.

He presents Himself as the bridegroom who has come.

## Authority over creation

Jesus' authority is not limited to religious matters.

In Mark 4, Jesus and His disciples are crossing the Sea of Galilee when a violent storm arises.

The disciples are terrified.

Jesus rebukes the wind and the sea, and the storm becomes calm.

The disciples respond:

> “Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?”
> — Mark 4:41 (NRSVA)

That question is the point.

Who is Jesus?

The Old Testament repeatedly presents God's sovereignty over the sea as an expression of His divine power.

Psalm 107 describes God stilling the storm and bringing the sailors safely to harbour.

Psalm 89 declares that God rules the raging sea.

Psalm 65 praises God as the one who stills the roaring of the seas.

Jesus does not pray for God to calm the storm.

He commands it.

And the storm obeys.

The Gospel does not immediately provide a theological explanation. Instead, it leaves the reader with the same question confronting the disciples:

**Who is this?**

## Jesus as judge

The Old Testament also presents God as the ultimate judge.

Yet Jesus speaks about Himself as the one who will judge humanity.

In Matthew 25, the Son of Man comes in glory, sits upon His throne and separates the nations as a shepherd separates sheep from goats.

The decisive question is how people have responded to Him and to those who belong to Him.

John's Gospel goes even further.

Jesus says that the Father has entrusted judgment to the Son and that all should honour the Son just as they honour the Father (John 5:22-23).

We do not need to repeat the entire passage here. The important point is that Jesus is not merely claiming that God will judge through Him as a passive instrument.

The Son Himself is presented as the one who exercises judgment.

That is a remarkable claim when read against the Old Testament background.

## Jesus receives worship

The Gospels also record people worshipping Jesus.

After Jesus walks on the water, those in the boat worship Him and say:

> “Truly you are the Son of God.”
> — Matthew 14:33 (NRSVA)

After His resurrection, the women take hold of His feet and worship Him (Matthew 28:9).

And at the end of Matthew's Gospel, the disciples worship the risen Christ (Matthew 28:17).

This is important because Scripture is very clear that worship belongs to God.

When Cornelius falls before Peter, Peter immediately tells him to stand because he is only a mortal (Acts 10:25-26).

When John attempts to worship an angel in Revelation, the angel refuses:

> “Worship God!”
> — Revelation 22:9 (NRSVA)

Jesus, however, receives worship.

The Gospels do not portray Him correcting those who worship Him.

That does not prove the entire doctrine of the Trinity by itself.

But it is another piece of the cumulative evidence.

## A pattern begins to emerge

Consider what the Gospels are presenting:

Jesus forgives sins.

Jesus claims lordship over the Sabbath.

Jesus identifies Himself with the bridegroom.

Jesus declares that something greater than the Temple is present in Him.

Jesus commands the wind and the sea.

Jesus is presented as the judge of humanity.

Jesus receives worship.

None of these claims should be isolated from its context.

Nor should we argue that every action Jesus performs could only possibly be done by God.

The stronger argument is cumulative.

**Jesus repeatedly occupies a position that, within the Jewish Scriptures, belongs uniquely to God.**

He does not simply speak about God.

He acts with God's authority.

## Is Jesus merely acting on God's behalf?

Someone might object that God frequently gives human beings authority to act on His behalf.

That is true.

Moses performs signs.

Elijah performs miracles.

Prophets announce God's forgiveness.

Kings exercise authority under God's appointment.

So the question is not whether God can work through a human representative.

He certainly can.

The question is whether Jesus fits that category.

And the Gospels keep pushing beyond it.

Jesus does not merely perform a miracle and then point away from Himself.

He repeatedly makes **Himself** the centre of the claim.

He forgives sins in His own authority.

He calls Himself Lord of the Sabbath.

He claims authority to judge.

He demands faith.

He receives worship.

He presents Himself as the bridegroom.

And He exercises authority over creation itself.

This is why the earliest Christians did not simply describe Jesus as a prophet who spoke for God.

They proclaimed Him as Lord.

The question is no longer:

**“Did Jesus ever say, ‘I am God’?”**

It becomes:

**“Why does Jesus repeatedly act and speak as though He possesses the authority of God Himself?”**

## Conclusion

The Gospels do not present Jesus' divinity through one isolated sentence.

Instead, they build a cumulative picture.

Jesus forgives sins.

He exercises authority over the Sabbath.

He presents Himself as the bridegroom.

He claims to be greater than the Temple.

He commands the forces of nature.

He exercises divine judgment.

And He receives worship.

These claims make sense only when we take seriously the Old Testament background against which Jesus lived and taught.

Jesus is not merely claiming to have **a message from God**.

He is repeatedly placing Himself at the centre of God's authority and God's work.

And that brings us to one of the most direct and controversial statements in John's Gospel:

> “The Father and I are one.”
> — John 10:30 (NRSVA)
