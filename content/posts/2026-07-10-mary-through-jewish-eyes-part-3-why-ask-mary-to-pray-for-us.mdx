# Why Ask Mary to Pray for Us?

If Catholics believe that Jesus Christ is the one mediator between God and humanity, why do they ask Mary to pray for them?

For many Christians, this practice seems unnecessary—or even unbiblical.

Yet Catholics see the answer in the very first miracle recorded in John's Gospel.

The Wedding at Cana is more than the story of water becoming wine.

Read through Jewish eyes, it reveals something profound about how God chooses to work through human cooperation.

## John Wants You to Notice Mary

One detail often goes unnoticed.

John never tells us the names of the bride or the groom.

That seems unusual.

If this were simply a historical account of a wedding, we would expect to know who was getting married.

Instead, John's attention is fixed almost entirely on Jesus and His mother.

The wedding itself almost fades into the background.

John is inviting the reader to look beyond the event itself and ask a deeper theological question.

What is this miracle trying to teach us?

## The Seventh Day of a New Creation

The opening chapters of John's Gospel mirror the opening chapters of Genesis.

Genesis begins:

> "In the beginning..."
>
> — Genesis 1:1 (NRSVA)

John deliberately echoes those same words:

> "In the beginning was the Word..."
>
> — John 1:1 (NRSVA)

Many scholars have noted that John's opening narrative unfolds across seven symbolic days.

The testimony of John the Baptist marks the first day.

Three successive "next day" references follow.

The Wedding at Cana begins:

> "On the third day..."
>
> — John 2:1 (NRSVA)

Four days plus three days.

Seven days.

John is presenting Jesus as inaugurating a new creation.

If the reader has already recognised Jesus as the New Adam and Mary as the New Eve, the miracle at Cana takes on an even richer meaning.

## Mary Notices the Need

John records Mary's first words:

> "They have no wine."
>
> — John 2:3 (NRSVA)

Mary simply presents the need.

She does not demand.

She does not command.

She entrusts the situation to Jesus.

His response appears surprising.

> "Woman, what concern is that to you and to me? My hour has not yet come."
>
> — John 2:4 (NRSVA)

Yet Mary is not discouraged.

Instead, she turns to the servants and says:

> "Do whatever he tells you."
>
> — John 2:5 (NRSVA)

These are the final recorded words of Mary in all of Scripture.

Remarkably, they contain no attention to herself.

Her final instruction is simply to obey her Son.

## Eve and Mary

The contrast with Genesis is striking.

The first woman listened to the serpent.

She then encouraged Adam to disobey God.

The consequences affected the whole human race.

At Cana, the New Eve leads others in exactly the opposite direction.

She calls them to obedience.

She points them toward Jesus.

Only after the servants obey Christ does the miracle occur.

The pattern has been reversed.

Where the first Eve became associated with humanity's first act of disobedience, Mary becomes associated with the first public sign of the New Adam.

## Why Catholics Ask for Mary's Prayers

Catholics see Cana as revealing a pattern that continues throughout salvation history.

People bring their needs to Mary.

Mary brings those needs to Jesus.

Jesus responds.

Mary directs everyone back to obedience to Christ.

Notice that Mary never becomes the destination.

She is always pointing beyond herself.

Everything she does leads to Jesus.

For this reason, Catholics ask Mary to pray for them in much the same way they ask fellow Christians to pray for them.

Her intercession never competes with Christ's unique role as Redeemer.

Rather, it participates in His saving work by leading people ever closer to Him.

## A Mother Who Leads to Her Son

Catholics sometimes describe Mary as the **Mediatrix of Grace**.

This title can easily be misunderstood.

It does not mean that Mary creates grace.

Nor does it place her alongside Jesus as another redeemer.

Every grace comes from Christ alone.

Yet God often delights in working through human cooperation.

At Cana, Jesus freely chooses to begin His public ministry after Mary's request.

Catholics see this not as a limitation on Christ's power but as a revelation of how He wishes to involve His people in His work.

The first public sign of Jesus' ministry arrives through the humble request of His mother.

## Looking Ahead

Mary appears only once more in John's Gospel.

Standing beneath the Cross, Jesus says something that has echoed throughout Christian history:

> "Woman, here is your son."

Then, turning to the beloved disciple:

> "Here is your mother."

What did Jesus mean?

And why has the Church understood this moment as establishing Mary's spiritual motherhood?

That is the question we will explore in Part 4.
